Why the middle layer matters

The equator isn't just one more coordinate — it's the hinge of the two-phase method. Try the moves below and watch the four slice edges. The phase-2 moves can never knock a slice edge out of the equator; the side-face quarter turns are the only moves that can.

The equator is the door to phase 2.

Kociemba's solver works in two phases. Phase 1 drives any scramble into a special subgroup G₁ = ⟨U, D, R2, L2, F2, B2⟩ — and one of the three conditions for being in G₁ is exactly that the four slice edges are back in the equator (the UD-slice coordinate is solved).

Inside G₁ you may only turn U, D and the half-turns R2, L2, F2, B2. Every one of these maps equator edges to equator edges — R2 just swaps FR↔BR, F2 swaps FR↔FL, and so on. So once the slice edges are home, they stay home for the rest of the solve. That closure is what makes phase 2 a small, self-contained search.

The quarter turns R, L, F, B are banned precisely because each one lifts its two slice edges up into the U/D layers — try one and the counter drops to 2/4.

phase 2    → finish within 19,508,428,800 states of G₁

That middle number — 2048·2187·495 — is the three workhorse coordinates multiplied together: edge orientation × corner orientation × UD-slice. The slice term is the part that says "the equator is populated correctly," so the middle layer is literally one of the three dials phase 1 has to zero out.

"The middle layer matters because it's the door to phase 2: get the slice edges home, and the rest of the cube can be finished without ever disturbing them again."
